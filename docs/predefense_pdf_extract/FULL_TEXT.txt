ABSTRACT
CKD (Chronic Kidney Disease) is a big problem all over the world, and is often only associated
with the affected kidney when it is at an advanced stage and there is not a lot that can be done.
Most of the computational approaches involve a unique modality, and do not typically combine
leakage-aware evaluation, and explainability in a single, reproducible pipeline for decision
support. In this thesis, multi-modal solution using clinical records, population biochemistry and
wearable physiology to predict the risk of CKD in the early stage using explainable AI is
proposed. Three branches are created: NHANES is used for modelling the population; MIMIC-
IV is used for admission level hospital risk stratification; and a proxy of stress-physiology
(WESAD) is used for feature extraction as the labels of CKD are not available in the WESAD
cohort. This also includes providing grouped splits at the subject level and providing
standardized feature engineering, and explicit labeling from population or from hospital proxies.
Calibrated logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, and random forests and tabular neural
networks have been tested; in late fusion, which does not involve any cross-cohort patient
alignment, the probability of the trees is combined by following a published protocol. The main
empirical finding is on the admission branch of the MIMIC: post-hoc calibration significantly
improves the probability reliability, compared with baselines that aren't calibrated, while
showing competitive discrimination. Global SHAP analysis on the MIMIC held-out test set
shows that the top four factors are the insurance, race, admission characteristics and age. With a
prototype built in Streamlit, we also introduced thresholds, and offer explanations to
stakeholders on a local and global level. While NHANES and WESAD branches were tested
separately, no significant CKD detection advantage of multi-modal fusion was found. The work
is a purely research decision support framework to target leakage-aware, calibrated and
explainable multi-modal hospital risk modelling.

Chapter 1
Introduction
The chapter sets out the motivation and aims of the project and outlines its methodology and expected
outcomes as well as formulating the overall structure of the project. It offers a glimpse into the early
Chronic Kidney Disease risk prediction problem and introduces a proposed explainable multi-modal
decision-support framework.
1.1
Introduction
CKD is a slowly progressive disease where kidney function decreases over a period of time as shown
by a declining eGFR or kidney damage in terms of structure or function [2], [18]. The severity of the
disease varies and can lead to end stage renal failure (ESRF), dialysis and transplant which is very
costly [2]. The prevalence of CKD is found worldwide, it often accompanies hypertension and type 2
diabetes and it is a condition that may not present with symptoms in the initial stages and therefore
not be diagnosed [2], [18]. Evidence of decreased kidney function or albuminuria early makes it
possible to intervene with meds and a modification of the diet and management of risk factors [17],
[18]. It is therefore a challenge for the nephrology and the preventive medicine community to be able
to identify high-risk persons so early.
Serum creatinine, eGFR using the CKD-EPI equation and urine ACR [17] have been the laboratory
markers traditionally used for the diagnosis of CKD. They are clinically useful, but are not continuous,
do not reflect the renal function, and are cross-sectional and dependent on time of day, muscle mass
and hydration, and also on the presence of other illnesses [1]. Laboratory screening is also hamstrung
by its intermittent lag time between clinic visits [2] and imaging has proven to be costly and less
available with the added problem of the inability to continuously monitor patients [2]. Thus, novel
diagnostic methods are needed that can detect any evolving alterations in the physiologic state before
renal dysfunction becomes evident, in this high-risk group.
There are new opportunities for using Artificial Intelligence (AI) in clinical risk prediction. Structured
and high dimensional patient data can be leveraged by analysing such data with machine learning or
deep learning algorithms, thus discovering complex nonlinear relationships which can supersede a
humanly-designed clinical scoring system in many cases [1], [13], [43]. Using deep learning on EHR
data has enabled better prediction of outcomes using temporal modelling on EHR data [8]. However,
the existing approaches to predict a CKD are mostly performed at present on one particular modality
of information, and fail to integrate complementary information from the other modality such as
wearable physiological sensors [1], [13], [32], [35]. Multi-modal wearable signals and clinical records
in structured format in the context of CKD remains an area that is yet to be explored in modelling [36].
However, another restriction is its interpretability. Clinicians in a critical clinical context would have
to view and understand the contributions of the inputs to the prediction, even in the event of good
discriminative performance of a model [46], [49], [50]. In the area of CKD prediction, understanding
feature attributions with explainable AI (XAI) methods and calibrated probabilistic outputs, and
framing them as decision support, are not sufficiently developed and remain as non-reproducible
elements of the same pipeline.
The next major challenge addressed in the research is the need for a multi-modal framework which
uses both EHR
and clinical data along with wearable, physiological signals, is able to obtain
estimates of personalized and calibrated risk scores, is explainable, and is feasible given lack of
patient-level alignment with public EHR, clinical, and non-clinical datasets. This thesis tries to solve
this issue by proposing a multi-modal deep learning framework, by connecting these data sources in a
probability level late fusion [36] and explainable with SHAP-based explainability [15] in order to
support transparent clinical decision making, instead of the autonomy of diagnosis [1], [42], [49].

1.2
Motivation
The prevalence of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is rising, as well as the lack of diagnosis of the
disease before the time at which clinical interventions can make a real difference in the world. This
change in renal function is a gradual process and in the early stages of CKD, there may be no
identifiable signs or symptoms; many of those at risk of being diagnosed with the condition will only
have nephron loss when diagnosed [2], [18]. This necessitates the demand for a predictive tool that
can be employed in identifying the high risk patients early on and manage and monitor them with
different types of drugs and changes in the lifestyle before taking them to end stage deterioration [17],
[18].
The workup for this is measurement of blood levels of creatinine, eGFR and urinary albumin to
creatinine ratio [17] periodically in the laboratory. The measurements are episodic and static, are
prone to non-renal effects and cannot be used in resource poor environments [1]. Current screening
systems can't keep up with patients in between screenings and lack much data, including a need for AI
systems that can process larger, more diverse data sets of patients.
The need for the work is related to the computation aspect arising from the intrinsic intractability
problem. When it comes to risk prediction of CKD, issues of high dimensionality of a sparse feature
space, non-existence of a cohort of patients with clinical data and physiological signals gathered from
wearable devices, heterogeneities of different data modalities and high degree of class imbalance all
pose challenges. New architectures introduced by these constraints require non-classical design of
classification and require principled design of architecture in deep learning models: (1) Late Fusion
and (2) Selection of the model based on the calibration. An important computation issue is how to
come up with solutions that can be applied to such real-world data sets.
Wearable devices provide continuous measurement of more general physiological parameters such as
BVP, EDA and skin temperature which have been demonstrated to be sensitive to more general
autonomic and cardiovascular measures relevant to chronic disease state [32], [35]. Use of multimodal
approach in combination with the structured data feature of EHR clinical can build up an enriched
predictive environment as compared to considering only one of them. But there are some practical
engineering problems to be addressed on a systematic basis, such as proxy label alignment, cross
dataset generalization and mismatching sampling rate, to mention a few.
There can be a number of direct hits to a problem's solution. But from a clinical point of view, if there
is a validated early detection tool, nephrologists can find the patients at risk before they start to show
symptoms, thus reducing dialysis burden, and improving the outcome of their quality of life [18], [43].
From a technical perspective, it can be seen as an extension of multimodal deep learning and SHAP
explainability to a relatively less explored field in healthcare that makes pipelines reproducible and
calibrates multimodal models to deliver clinically interpretable or relevant values to the burgeoning
field of explainable healthcare AI models [15], [49]. The project aims to enable the research team
immediately to extend their capabilities in a field where there is a dire demand among both academic
researchers and practitioners of AI in healthcare specially in clinical data engineering as well as deep
learning model design and XAI systems.
1.3
Objectives
The aim of the research is to develop an explainable machine learning and a Deep Learning
framework able to predict the risk of CKD patients from the very early stage with the assistance of
wearable generated physiological data and structured clinical data and a final system that instead of
being an automatic diagnostic system, works as a system able to support clinical decisions.
I.
To prepare and preprocess CKD-relevant clinical datasets from NHANES and MIMIC-IV,
including feature selection, missing-value handling, leakage removal, CKD label construction,
and grouped train-validation-test splitting.
II.
To extract and prepare wearable physiological features from the WESAD dataset using window-
based signal processing for the wearable branch of the system.
III. To develop and compare branch-level prediction models for clinical and EHR data, including
logistic regression with calibration, Random Forest, XGBoost, Tabular MLP, and residual MLP

not a clinically proven same patient multimodal CKD detector.
1.4
Methodology
This research starts with a pipeline that begins with three publicly available datasets: The NHANES
dataset, a population level dataset, in which the primary focus is on clinical data [16], the MIMIC-IV
dataset, a hospital-based EHR dataset [6] and the WESAD dataset with a primary focus on wearable
physiology modalities [5]. All the data in each of these datasets were separately preprocessed and
labels of CKD were created using clinical criteria; subject level leakage was avoided by sampling the
data in to three such set of training, validation and testing [17], [18]. For each branch clinical features
were extracted from the structured data and statistical features were extracted cross-sectionally
windowing the data of the wearable signals. The discrimination and calibration performances of these
models were used for model selection as several supervised learning models: deep tabular models,
Logistic regression with the probability calibration, Random Forest model and XGBoost model, were
trained at each branch. Level fusion with quality fusion based on normalized predictions performances
[36] was performed to fuse the predictions of different modalities. To gain clinical transparency from
the main clinical model, SHAP-based explainability was added to the model to produce explainable
feature attribution scores towards that end [15], [48]. The AUROC score, AUPRC score, F1 score,
calibration error and robustness were used to evaluate system performance with different experimental
conditions.
1.5
Project Outcome
The outcome of the research will be a simple system to detect the early CKD risk from the structured
clinical data, hospital EHR data and wearable data. This system will not be used as medical diagnosis
system.
The accuracy, AUROC, F1-Score, Specificity, Recall and ECE is 69.2%, 0.7614, 0.4145, 0.6959,
0.6739 and 0.0203 respectively of the primary calibrated MIMIC-IV model on held-out test set. This
best model of NHANES clinical branch achieved an accuracy of 96.60%, AUROC of 0.9922 and F1
score of 0.7975. This proxy Stress vs Baseline task was able to achieve an accuracy of the wearable of
81.21%, an AUROC value of 0.7639 and F1-Score of the standard value 0.6667
The project also prepared a late-fusion combination framework which can be tailored to each branch
on the basis of a concept labeled as probability of right prediction. However, the datasets have not
been same-patient aligned, and as there currently are no CKD labels available in the WESAD data,
fusion is a research outcome evaluated, and for this reason a clinically-validated multimodal CKD
system cannot be considered yet.
The reproducible CKD prediction pipeline, branch assessed models, calibrated main disease model,
explainability via SHAPs, multimodal fusion and prototype decision support interface are the outputs
of end-to-end.

Chapter 2
Background
The key area of focus of the chapter is the literature background and context investigation where
important literature work that will form a vital base for the proposed CKD risk prediction framework
is explored. It covers the cutting edge prediction of CKD, AI in health care from EHR (Electronic
Health Records), Wearables to monitor in health care, Multimodal learning, explainable AI and
related prior works and not yet explored research gaps in this project.
2.1
Introduction
Because it is not easily diagnosed and only picked up in later stages of the condition, there are many
people around the world who suffer from Chronic Kidney Disease, in particular those living with
moderate to severe kidney damage. Chronic kidney disease is under-diagnosed in the early phases of
the condition due to the limitations of time on time laboratory based screening, although it affects a
significant proportion of adults around the world. Much research has been devoted to the application
of machine learning and deep learning approaches to clinical data to predict the risk of CKD, with a
pattern from basic classifiers based on structured records, to more complicated architectures based on
EHR and time series data [1], [7], [23]. Imaging-based CKD approaches have also been reviewed [4],
[12], but they are outside the scope of this tabular and wearable physiology framework. Current
strategies are mostly based on only one data type and do not integrate complementary data provided
by different types of wearable physiological sensors that are capable of providing continuous real-
time measurements that are relevant for monitoring patient's chronic disease [32], [36]. Additionally,
deep learning models have not been sufficiently discussed in terms of interpretability in the clinical
practice [46], [49], which is the only criterion required to apply deep learning in practice despite their
outstanding predictive power. The chapter covered literature on the above aspects and stated the
limitations of existing system and the research gaps which trigger to develop the proposed system.
2.2
Literature Review
The previous methods focused to handle structured clinical data and use the traditional classification
algorithms of machine learning for prediction of CKD. Sawhney et al. [1] compared the performance
of deep models and classical models using a UCI style CKD data composed of 400 patients and some
clinical/laboratory attributes. They had used PyTorch with their deep MLP technique and the
technique achieved 100% test accuracy, achieving a better performance compared to the SVM and
Naive Bayes method. The results are only applicable to a small size, carefully designed tabular set,
and cannot be directly applied to a more diverse set or extended to a more class balanced and missing
EHR set. Almansour et al. [19] compared the performance of ANN with SVM for the same data set of
400 patients with CKD and found that the accuracy of ANN (99.75%) is much better than that SVM
(97.75%) while the most significant is creatinine (cr) and urea (urea). The latter three works [20], [21],
[22] by Wang, Shih and Krishnamurthy respectively, employed other additional feature engineering
approaches but were limited in their cross-sectional data from single acquisition sites and not taking
into account temporal changes, which would be important to capture the true complexities of clinical
CKD progression. Some attempts were made to indicate that selection of appropriate features is very
crucial for achieving high classification accuracy in case of class imbalance condition [11] and
Dashtban et al. [13] showed that one of the drawbacks of these classifiers when experimented on new
cohorts other than the experimented cohorts is fragility at population shift. Khan et al. [10] compared
several machine learning techniques—including tree-based methods such as J48 and NBTree—on a
UCI CKD dataset and reported that CHIRP achieved the highest classification accuracy (99.75%)

Extremely robust predictive models were developed when deep learning was applied to EHR data.
Developed by Miotto et al. [24] is a three-layer stacked denoising autoencoder (DeepPatient) trained
on approximately 700,000 patient records given by Mount Sinai that achieved better AUC-ROC
values, ranging from 0.659 for the unprocessed feature baseline to 0.773, demonstrating a 15%
improvement along with better accuracy (15%) and F score (54%). Choi et al. [25] proposed Doctor
AI, an attention-based recurrent neural network model that has a structure of two-level attention
which was tested on 14 million clinical visits from 263,000 patients with the AUROC of heart failure
prediction as 0.8705, 0.8256 for MLP and 0.7900 for logistic regression respectively. Lipton et al. [29]
used a MIMIC clinical time series data to test the performance of LSTM networks against that of
plain zero imputation for prediction of multiple labels associated with the diagnosis of the patient, and
showed that it could outperform the MLP baselines when trained with hand-curated features with a
best micro-averaged AUROC of 0.8730. A deep learning model by Rajkomar et al. [23] with over 46
billion ESR data points from two hospital systems was able to achieve AUROC of 0.75–0.77 for the
prediction of 30-day readmission; the model of 0.85–0.86 for the prediction of a long length of stay
and 0.93–0.95 for the prediction of inpatient mortality outperformed the traditional clinical models
and had a frequency-weighted AUROC of 0.90 for discharge diagnosis. Many of the challenges of
utilizing clinical deep learning are still open and underscored by Shickel et al. [28] with the most
obvious challenges being: temporal modelling of irregular longitudinal recording, handling
missingness and generalizing across cohorts. Although all these improvements have been made, all
the DL models presented here in this paper were found to be single-modality and no attempts were
made to fuse extra-hospital physiological signals.
Wearable health monitoring devices carry with them a qualitatively different type of data with regard
to physiological information; the data is continuous, longitudinal and taken in real life. This study, by
Dunn et al. [32] used more than 150 derived features of these vitals and trained two different models,
random forest and LASSO, to achieve the following: a longitudinal study of 54 patients showed that
these wearables can predict clinical lab measurements (e.g. hematocrit, hemoglobin, red blood cell
count, and absolute monocyte count) with lower prediction error. Presently observed finding is
pertinent to this area of CKD as anemia is quite common complication with renal insufficiency and
hematocrit and hemoglobin are indicators of anemia. Banaee et al. [35] explored the existing data
mining algorithms suitable to the data streams generated by the wearables sensors and how they can
be adapted into useful algorithms to detect the anomalies in the physiological data to monitor chronic
diseases. Steinhubl et al. [34] envisioned that usage of mobile health would be on-going and would
provide detection of clinically relevant changes not captured during clinics visits. Consumer wearable
modalities are signals that have been studied by Bent et al [33] who have pointed out that the
physiology-state-estimation is best achieved on signals such as BVP, EDA and ST. Later on,
Binsawad [9] did a research work on prediction of CKD using Optimized Forest model and compared
it with several traditional machine learning models as baseline which showed sensitivity and
specificity values more than several baseline models on the features extracted using the ECG signals.
It shows that even non-WESAD signals like ECG, known as its inputs can be used for a renal risk
model especially in absence of structured labels at the level of the sensor. A challenge that faced all
the wearables studies of CKD analyzed was that there were no validated CKD labels that made use of
the wearables technology, and researchers were forced to resort to proxy tasks, like stress or CVS
detection.
A capability that comes up as one of the identified opportunities is the one of combining multiple
datasets using a multitude of modalities, when it comes to AI in healthcare context. A detailed
taxonomy of multimodal machine learning was put forward by Baltrušaitis et al. [36] which
categorizes approaches in three types such as approaches with a fusion in the representation space,
approaches with translation, approaches with data alignment, and approaches with co-learning.
Patient-level data alignment as a key practical challenge in the deployment of multimodal models.
One of the earliest works to indicate that multi-modal training of the same model with still images,
audio, and video can be useful to enhance feature representations was that provided by Ngiam et al.
[37]. However, there are no such systems with a specific interest to combine structured clinical
records present in EHRs with wearable physiological signals in prediction of risks of CKD, showing a
clear problem that motivates the proposed work [1], [13].

For clinical applications the explainability of the AI systems are now an important prerequisite. In this
paper, inspired by so called Shapley values from cooperative game theory, we present one approach
called SHAP which was developed by Lundberg and Lee [15] that can be used for all types of models
and give theoretically motivated locally consistent feature attributions. Recently, Ribeiro et al. [44]
proposed LIME for providing surrogate explanation which is somehow trustworthy. For the
convolutional models, Selvaraju et al. [45] have proposed to use gradient-weighted visual
explanations named as Grad-CAM. Tree based model classes like the models in this thesis can be
directly used in SHAP, or TreeSHAP [48] can be used to give exact polynomial time computation.
According to Tonekaboni et al. [46] clinicians need to rely not only on uncertainty estimates but also
on feature attributions; otherwise it is not clinically useful and relying on feature level attributions
would not work during a high stake situation. Just as with any other model explanation editing, the
goal of the model interpretation should be to understand how the model operates, so it could be used
to inform a decision making process, and to ensure that the model has learned correct association,
even an interpretable model can learn incorrect association in the clinical data context and this was
illustrated by Caruana et al. [47]. There are two main problems, however, that impede the current AI
prediction tools from their clinical use, miscalibration of the probability as identified by both
Ghassemi et al. [49] and Vellido [50].

Chapter 3
Research Methodology
This chapter presents the research methodology adopted for the proposed CKD risk prediction
framework, describing the datasets, preprocessing, feature extraction, model development, multimodal
fusion, explainability, and evaluation design. It explains how each methodological stage connects to
the overall goal of building an explainable, multimodal, decision-support system for early CKD risk
prediction.
3.1
Methodology
A structured research methodology was performed to develop the proposed CKD risk prediction
framework, which clearly aids the research purpose. The methodology goes hand-in-hand with a
multi-branch pipeline. Three types of data, population clinical, hospital EHR data and wearable
physiological data, are all independently modelled and processed, and their respective predictions
directly combined at the probability level. Goal is to get every stage of this pipeline clearly presented
and reproducible from data acquisition through to preprocessing, feature extraction, model
development, fusion, explainability and evaluation. One stage is then described in detail in each of the
subsections. As far as possible, the same methodology is adhered to throughout with the code adopted
and the experimental set-up locked, to represent the system as constructed, and to meet the
requirement of an explainable multimodal decision support tool for early prediction of CKD.
3.1.1
Overview
The research activity proposed is a stride towards the effort of early detection of patients who are
prone to developing Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) which has no symptom in the initial stage of the
disease. For this purpose, the chapter builds upon three different, yet complementary health data types
and processes them through an explainable and reproducible workflow to a prototype of an
explainable decision support tool.
Three kinds of data were utilized: Population clinical data (NHANES); hospital EHR data (MIMIC-
IV); and portable wearable, physiological data (WESAD). These data-set are classified as individual
branches and are preprocessed, feature preparation and predictive-models are found for each one of
these data-sets. In each of the branches, machine learning and deep learning models were developed
and compared with one another and discrimination and probabilistic calibration was performed, which
might lead to the selection of models capable of giving reliable risk estimates.
Finally all the branch outputs are fused via a performance weighted method, that is a calibrated risk
probability of each branch is fused. To achieve the transparency, a complementary explainability
modular component is added to the main model based on SHAP and it generates feature level
explanations, implemented as a prototype decision support system instead of diagnostic tool [15].
3.1.2
Proposed Methodology
This methodology we are proposing is a multi-branch workflow, that converts various types of health
data that can be collected to calibrated explainable indexes of the risk of CKD as represented in
Figure 3.1. The design process for each stage is briefly described here.


============================================================

============================================================

Figure 3.1: Overall architecture of the proposed multimodal CKD risk-prediction framework
There are three kinds of public data sources that provide data: population-scale clinical data collected
by NHANES [16] and hospital EHR data from MIMIC-IV [6] and wearable physiological data
collected by WESAD public data sharing repository [5]. Given that they represent different
populations and cannot be connected on an individual patient basis, each is considered a separate
branch, and follows its own preprocessing steps which involves table merging, leakage removal,
imputation, standardization and train – validation – test splitting to prevent subject leakage, using
CKD-EPI eGFR and albuminuria thresholds [17], [18]. We then have modality specific features such
as structure admitting, modality specific demographic features on the clinical branch, windowed
statistical descriptors of BVP, EDA, temperature, and accelerometer features on the wearable branch,
which we use as a proxy for a CKD labeled source.
For each branch, there are a number of models, including logistic regression with calibration, Random
Forest and XGBoost and deep tabular models; the locked model in the EHR branch is the logistic
regression with calibration which is used to generate well calibrated probabilities [14]. These
probabilities at the branches are then combined at the level of the probabilities as an integration
protocol like that of the non-aligned data [36]. A SHAP-based-component reports feature attributions,
discrimination and calibration metrics such as AUROC, AUPRC, F1, sensitivity, specificity, brier and
ECE are employed to assess the system with a multi-seed and a temporal robustness check.
The system is divided into four blocks, which are respectively input and pre-processing, model
inference with the frozen primary model, SHAP explanation and explanation presentation interface
from system design perspective. The data is only transmitted from the computer to the user only one
way; the system returns a calibrated CKD risk estimate and supporting explanation outputs to the user;
inputs are admission-level features entered or selected through the prototype interface.

3.1.4
Data Flow Diagram
The Data Flow Diagram illustrates the flow of data from various external data sources into the
proposed framework for CKD prediction with outputs for prediction, explanation, storage and
reporting. The main external entities include the public datasets and system user. NHANES supplies
population-level clinical information, MIMIC-IV supplies hospital EHR and laboratory information
and WESAD supplies wearable physiological information. A user only interacts with the user
interface of the prototype and asks for input features, and they will get prediction and explanation
outputs.
The inputs consist of mainly clinical and demographic features obtained from the NHANES,
admission level EHR and laboratory features obtained from the MIMIC-IV, and wearable signal
windows obtained from the WESAD. The inputs go through an additional layer of data ingestion, data
preprocessing, and feature extraction, and then the branch-level prediction. Processed feature sets for
modelling and also the model check points for the trained model for prediction. Because the data sets
are not patient-aligned, the fusion process isn't patient-aligned either, meaning that in cases where
there is a valid output, only branch-level probabilities are combined.
Both processes, calibrated CKD risk prediction and SHAP-based explainability, return outputs:
namely risk probabilities and feature-importance outputs and SHAP-based attributions. These results
are saved along with evaluation and fusion results and displayed using a prototype interface. The
results to be produced as the final outputs of the project are arrangement of CKD risk scores,
explanation results, reporting outputs for the research evaluation and demonstration for the decision
support, but not for autonomous diagnosis.

3.2
Detailed Methodology and Design
This section describes in detail methodology and design of the proposed multi modal CKD decision –
support framework. Highlighted key design decisions with regards to the selection of datasets, pre-
processing and leakage control, construction of labels, feature extraction, training of models and
calibration of the probability, late fusion, explainability and the research prototype. For each area, a
brief description of the selected approach, a description of any approaches considered but not selected,
a justification for excluding those approaches and significant compromises that were made between
validity of the approach, computational cost, usability and interpretability of results and suitability to
the research in clinical decision support are provided.
3.2.1
Dataset Description
The framework takes advantage of three datasets publicly available and consists of three modalities.
The population level clinical and demographic data are derived from NHANES (2013-2014) which
consists of 4702 model participants with 74 features, with 6.55% of participants labelled CKD-
positive of kidney disease patients having eGFR<60 mL/min/1.73 m2, with the CKD-EPI 2021 race-
free equation [16], [17], [18]. MIMIC-IV v3.1 contains the data in the hospital Electronic Health
Record (EHR), which include ~30,000 hospital admissions with 68 features with a positive rate of
~15.23%, including 8 measurements obtained from the hospitals laboratories in the window of
hospital admission (bicarbonate, chloride, creatinine, hemoglobin, platelet, potassium, sodium, and
urea nitrogen values [6]. WESAD is a dataset comprising of wrist worn data from 15 participants with


============================================================
PAGE 30
============================================================
20
each time-series divided into 578 windows with features from blood volume pulse (BVP),
electrodermal activity (EDA), skin temperature (TEMP), and accelerometer (ACC) [5].
To this end three existing publicly available datasets around population, hospital and physiological
modalities will be leveraged. The only other alternative considered was obtaining a collection of a
single, proprietary cohort of the three modalities for the same patients, but this was not selected
because there was no publicly available cohort and, because of the time frame and ethical concerns of
a final year design project, it was deemed impractical to collect such a cohort. A second alternative,
which was MIMIC only, was discarded as it would drown out wearing populations and sizes of
interest that motivated the multimodal research question, but were substantially only MIMIC, even
though MIMIC was not the main branch of the prototype for reporting and decision support built with
the lock. The good news about the public data sets is that they are reproducible, large and have an
ethical approval; the bad news and critical limitation is that the three types of data sets are from
different types of populations and cannot be connected at an individual patient level. All of these data
are fit for a relatively small computer. Clinically, the data sets assembled are for a population
screening as well as an inpatient risk and a chronic physiology for monitoring, but with no patient
overlap, only a multimodal methodology and integration protocol are apparent, as is the open
reporting style throughout.
The data of each cohort is divided into three sets a training set, a validation set, and a test set, and the
ratio of splitting is dependent on the cohort; NHANES split 60:20:20; WESAD split 75:25, because
it's a proxy branch for physiology without CKD labels.

3.2.2
Data Preparation
A separate pipeline is used to process each of the datasets. In NHANES, the race-free CKD-EPI 2021
equation is used to calculate outcomes, with eGFR thresholds used to construct labels, outcome
highly-leaking columns such as raw creatinine and identifier columns that are used to create the label
are removed, values are median imputed, and a grouped train:validation:testing split of 60:20:20 is
used. The records for MIMIC-IV are merged on the basis of subject and admission id; for each of the
eight laboratory features, processing is done on the admission window, the processing transformations
are fit on the training partition of the data, and then applied to the validation and test data [6]. For
WESAD, the window size is set to 1920 samples, the stride is set to 960 samples, no more than 120
windows per subject will be segmented and the windows will be split using a 75/25 train–test partition.
The chosen method is grouped splitting which is labelling using a clinical approach. Random splitting
was also considered but rejected as it can give optimistic and clinically unrealistic performance
estimates due to the presence of records of the same subject or participant in multiple splits; The
problem of grouped splitting reduces the flexibility in the balance of the rare positive classes across
the splits but in the context of assessing a decision-support tool it is salient that the performance be
judged on unseen individuals [27]. The imputation method selected was chosen to be median
imputation as it is stable, transparent, and less likely to have information leakage between validation
portions and imputation models; however, it does not model feature correlations. In addition, the
NHANES targets are based on the creatinine cutoffs of the guideline-based staging logic and the
CKD-EPI labelling system was selected to avoid the use of arbitrary creatinine cutoffs. In the

NHANES cycles when the primary rule is applied, the availability of albuminuria in merged
NHANES tables was limited to consider an alternative rule of eGFR alone, or eGFR and albuminuria
thresholds. These steps are very inexpensive and can be repeated in the computer. Determinate
processing with explicit rules to be checked and audited during deployment can be helpful as every
transformation can be versioned and replayed on new admissions without being re-derived because of
missing documentation of the rules.
3.2.3
Feature Engineering
Engineering of features is modality specific. All the fields in the clinical use and EHR feature parts
are structured, including demographic, admission, insurance, race, gender, length of stay, and
laboratory; the categorical variables are one-hot, the numerical variables are train-only fitted and
standardized. The wearable branch window process is to transform raw signal data into statistical
information that is mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values of data in each window
separately extracted from BVP, EDA, TEMP and Accelerometer magnitude signals.
Selected approach prefer interpretable features, structured features and hand-summarized, wearable
windows. An end-to-end convolutional or recurrent signal encoder was considered for modelling the
wearable branch, but was rejected due to the small size of the WESAD dataset and lack of CKD labels,
which would lead to an encoder overfitting and being unable to be validated against a renal outcome
[5], [29]. Even another idea, which was to pass the raw sequence whose length is fixed directly to the
deep network was rejected for the same reasons of amount of data and mismatch of effects of different
labels. The benefits of the statistical window features are both their robustness and the fact that they
only have a few parameters; the drawback is that subtle temporal evolutions and cross channel
interactions in the window may be washed out. Windowed is lightweight computation and
programmer-friendly for the deployment of prototypes on typical hardware. On a clinical level, the
structured and interpretable features are consistent with practitioners understanding and reasoning
about laboratory information and summary vital signs, and the features on the wearable are a proxy
for physiological stress, but not a labelled predictor of CKD.

3.2.4
Multimodal Fusion Strategy
A late fusion of the outputs of the branches is made at probability level. Each branch makes a class
prediction, and the aggregated branch predictions are done using weights based on normalized
AUROC and AUPRC statistics; an alternative combiner, called a logistic-regression meta-learner on
branch probabilities, is also evaluated.
The approach selected is late fusion and the reason for this is that the datasets are not patient-aligned.
There were other options considered, early feature-level fusion and joint end-to-end fusion networks
for multiple modalities, which could in principle learn the modalities' interaction in principle on the
row level but were rejected because these would require creating patient equivalences in the data that
were used for fusion, and would be fabricated, in violation of the no-fabrication principle of this study.
A single-model alternative in which only the features of the MIMIC EHR was put into consideration,
and serves as the backbone of the locked hospital model and Streamlit prototype, but because of its
inability to address the multimodal integration objective, it was not selected as a separate research
design. The positive side of late fusion is that it works without loss of functionality under a structural
constraint of independent cohorts, it is independently trainable, auditable and replaceable and also has
very low inference cost as fusion is taking place in the probability space. The downside is that, like, if
you have aligned data, then there could be cross-modal feature interactions that a joint representation
could learn but it can't learn here due to late fusion, this is explicitly stated. These modularities are
clinically and deployable beneficial: the hospital, the population and the wearable branches can
independently be improved when new data occurs, but due to the lack of an aligned cohort, it is not a
clinically validated multimodal renal diagnosis system, but rather a deployable fusion protocol
reported under proxy conditions.
3.2.5
Model Architecture and Selection
Several models were explored for each of the tabular branches: calibrated logistic regression, Random
Forest (400 trees, max depth 16, weighted 0.5 subsample for each class), XGBoost (400 estimators,
max depth 5, learning rate 0.05, histogram method, positive class weighted), tabular MLP (128/64
hidden units, ReLU, dropout 0.2, AdamW 1 × 10-3), and tabular Residual Network (128-dim
projection, three layer-normalized residual blocks, AdamW 8 × 10-4, 50 epochs). Random Forest
window-level statistics on wearable branch. The locked primary model for the hospital EHR branch
was logistic regression with Platt (sigmoid) calibration of the validation prediction.
The downside of the selected approach would be the training of deep tabular networks as opposed to
good tree ensembles and the linear model as the main deep MIMIC classifier that is always calibrated.
Recurrent and transformer-based EHR sequence models were considered, but end to end sequence
models were not since EHR sequence modelling has been successfully applied in longitudinal studies
[25], [28] but were felt to be very susceptible to over-fitting in this small cohort with a tabular
admission-level representation where laboratories were already aggregated within the admission
window, and do not substantially improve upon strong tabular baselines [23], [24]. The tree
ensembles and deep networks that comprised the trained tabular models however gave slightly better
ranking on the MIMIC test partition while calibrated logistic regression was much better, especially
with regard to the most important criterion of ECE which is essential for a risk oriented decision
support [14], [47]. For the wearable branch, Random Forest was the better algorithm as it can learn
nonlinear patterns from low-dimensional window statistics without requiring large CKD-labelled
wearable cohorts. The advantage of the main model selected is the low serving time, low and stable
probabilities and easy understandable; the disadvantage is the slightly lower ranking results as
compared with the optimal nonlinear models. Going on the positive side, the linear primary model is
very cheap to train and deploy, which helps in the prototyping of Streamlit, and any batch-scoring
pipeline generated at the end of the model trainings. Clinically calibrated (not just "good rank")
probabilities are the appropriate way to prioritize for decision support, since the clinician's goal is to
have a good estimate of the risk – see [14] for details.

3.2.6
Explainable AI Integration
With the help of SHAP, the prototype introduces explainability and, per case, the user can view its
linear contribution of the features displayed on the admission [15] [48].
SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is the approach selected. The other main local explanation
methods that were considered here were LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) [44]
which introduces local surrogate sampling that can lead to flaky behavior when sampling different
perturbations and simulation runs, but that was not desired when implementing the pipeline for the
linear and tree based models explored here one can compute exact or semi-exact SHAP [15], [48] that
provide locally consistent attributions based on a common additive feature-attribution framework.
Although features level SHAP was discussed for tabular deep models by comparing against feature-
level Grad-CAM and attention level visualization, it was deprioritized due to the linear nature of the
locked primary model as well as the structured nature and nameability of the clinical features from
imaging. The most appealing aspect of SHAP is that it provides faithful, comparable explanations at
both the individual and global level which can be easily used for auditing individual admissions and
for the drivers at the cohort level in the MIMIC branch. Unfortunately, it comes at the price of more
computation compared to the single surrogate fit, but is well tolerated for the number and size of
features and models. Feature-level attributions are used flexibly, in conjunction with domain
knowledge, which is important to achieve successful adoption of decision-support systems [39], [49],
[50]; in the prototype interface, the attributions are precomputed on the global level and light on a
local level, meaning that they are quickly generated when the interface is displayed. One disadvantage
is that explanations only tell of relationships present in the training distribution, and they are not
inherently causal mechanisms and do not replace the medical doctors' judgment [49].

In the end, the chosen approach has provided the necessary rigorous train–validation–test split, which
has split the subjects or participants in groups based on their levels. Random splitting was considered,
since it would result in all the optimistic, clinically unrealistic estimates of performance given by
having multiple records of the same person on multiple partitions [27]. In particular, Sigmoid
calibration was favoured over isotonic calibration, since the isotonic method is more flexible but has a
greater tendency to over-fit on smaller validation sets, and has a more simpler calibration curve [14].
In this study, class balancing (class_weight) and the ensemble was also tested and used to prevent the
naive unweighted training of the models, although the proportion of CKD was low in NHANES and
MIMIC, a naive unweighted training would bias the model towards the majority negative class,
decreasing the sensitivity for screening use [47]. On top of that, it was suggested to do the extensive
experimental auto search but this was not done due to the time and the computation involved.
Reproducibility was maintained through fixed, documented experimental configurations rather than
extensive automated hyperparameter search. The upside of this approach is less optimism and no
reporting artifacts and reality based probability of threshold policies; the down side of this approach is
some loss of performance due to the limited search. Easy on the calculations; can be repeated using
the same workstation. Finally, the frozen calibrated model and the objects to be pre-processed are
loaded directly for inference to ensure the same results for the entire notebook pipeline and Streamlit
prototype.

4.1.1
Hardware and Software Environment
Development and evaluation have been done on a local workstation with Python 3.12, in an isolated
virtual environment and used a Jupyter Notebook tool as a reproducible experimentation environment
for development. The implementation stack consisted of pandas and NumPy for data manipulation;
scikit-learn for data-preprocessing, classical models and calibration; PyTorch for tabular deep
networks and baseline methods using gradient-boosted models (XGBoost); and Streamlit for the
prototype of the decision-support system. Often tabular deep models were found to be useful and were
deployed with a specific GPU, but if one didn't exist these models ran on CPU, hence the locked

Primary MIMIC admission model has been analysed to check for explainability of the result using the
tool SHAP. All models were trained and the entire data sequence was exported to a serialized format

4.1.2

Testing and Evaluation
4.2.1
Validation Strategy
The model validation process was designed and developed on a staged approach, in order to provide
to a certain extent a level of independence and to reduce potential optimism bias in the process and
ultimately in provision of decision support reports. The methodological approach that was used was
grouped holdout evaluation with these separation performed as disjoint(user level grouping) and
disjoint(subject level grouping), respectively between NHANES and MIMIC-IV. The training and
validation data were used for pre-processing the data, fitting the model, calibration and setting
thresholds while the held out test partition was used for a final evaluation.
3 different validation designs were added to the main branch of the hospital. With repeating the
experiments some times each time with different random-seeds for the subject-groups, the stability of
the performance with the resampled subject-groups was investigated. For temporal external validation,
the subjects were divided into training,validation and test groups according to the first admission and
models of the training group were used for the prediction of the test admission to approximate the
temporal generalization. From the inability to perform the experiments at the patient-level between
NHANES, MIMIC-IV, and WESAD, the multimodal integration path was assessed using a branch
level proxy, and as such the multimodal integration results represent a demonstration of integration
protocol, rather than proof of multimodal CKD detection in a prospective setting.
To study the window records of the WESAD, a proxy label was adopted and used separately in the
wearable branch. The NHANES and MIMIC databases were independent; therefore, the patient level
testing was carried out separately for the two databases.
4.2.2
Evaluation Metrics
The performance of models were evaluated in a combination of discrimination, classification and
calibration aspects. To take the imbalanced CKD related labels into account, discrimination was
assessed based on area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under
the precision, recall curve (AUPRC). The accuracy, F1-score, sensitivity and specificity at the
selected operating points in the validation were used to classify the threshold. The quality of
calibration was assessed using the expected calibration error (ECE) and brier score since it is
important that probabilities are trustworthy in order to make decisions based on risks [14], [47]. For
locked primary hospital model, the number of items counted is from the held out test set which is
shown in the confusion matrix. Explainability was measured with the help of the SHAP of the main
classifier [15], [48].
4.2.3
Testing Procedures
Testing was set up at 5 steps of the procedure. Data-integrity testing was conducted and integrity of
the split and collective grouping, along with the integrity of imputation/scaling being applied only to
the training data, was confirmed. In this model-comparison test all the candidate classifiers were
trained in the same manner with same pre-processing method and a comparison of classifiers was
done on the validation data before reporting the test-set results. All three of the models – the
uncalibrated hospital logistic regression model, the model calibrated by sigmoid function, and the
model calibrated by isotonic method were compared and locked the primary reporting model. Various


============================================================
PAGE 40
============================================================

threshold policies were examined using operating point testing including the maximising F1 selection
policy, the maximising recall policy and thresholding based on validation predictions according to
Youden's method. Explainability and prototype testing assessed global SHAP consistency of locked
model and functional behavior of Streamlit decision-support prototype for calibrated risk display,
threshold adjustment and for providing explanations on the MIMIC branch only.
All the above results were determined numerically by the model and provided branch-wise,
performances of the main model, robust validation, temporal validation, proxy on the wearable, fusion
protocol assessment, SHAP rankings.

A Streamlit prototype was used to assess the locked MIMIC branch, as a research demonstration. The

4.3.10
Discussion
However, the findings, when combined, suggest three conclusions in terms of this study.
Firstly, sigmoid-calibrated logistic regression, on the primary branch of admission to MIMIC,
achieved a sensible trade-off between discrimination and reliability. It attained AUROC 0.761 with
ECE 0.020, while similar results were obtained with logistic regression with AUROC 0.761 and ECE
0.249 (no calibration), and AUROC ~0.768 with ECE ~0.20 with the tree-based models. So,
calibrated probabilities should be used instead of only ranking performance to support decisions.
Second, the NHANES branch demonstrated very high population discrimination (0.992) which should
not be assumed as hospital admission discrimination. There are differences in setting, in definition of
label, in features available and their rates of use, and results from the branches should not be averaged
or merely applied to the inpatient setting.
Third, the wearable and fusion components remain methodological rather than clinically validated
CKD detectors. WESAD was evaluated using a stress-versus-baseline proxy because CKD labels
were unavailable in that cohort, while NHANES and MIMIC-IV used branch-specific CKD-related
labels under different settings. Late fusion across disjoint public cohorts also did not show patient-
level multimodal CKD benefit. These parts therefore describe a reproducible multimodal design under

structural data constraints, not a deployed diagnostic system.
Overall, the project is best thought of as a research framework for branch-level leakage-aware,
calibrated and explainable hospital admission risk-stratification, in view of branch-level evidence,
SHAP review, and a Streamlit decision-support prototype. Before deployment to the real world, there
would be need for prospective clinical validation as well as same patient multimodal data.
4.4
Summary
A testing and evaluation infrastructure was introduced as a stepping stone to the implementation of
the proposed multimodal CKD risk prediction framework and empirical results as part of the proposed
framework was introduced in this chapter. This software stack, defensive design grouping and
discrimination metrics, model calibration metrics and a phased testing process were also experimented
with prior to reporting. Last, and certainly not least, a model, the sigmoid calibrated logistic
regression, is a locked model that might have been applied in the main MIMIC cohort of admissions
and these models are able to provide just as good, and indeed far better, baselines with much better
probability reliability level. A more intensive patient-level validations of the multimodal approach
were required, however, as well as limited usage of the proxy which is worn by patients with the
WESAD system. Using SHAP analysis and Streamlit prototype, the explanation of the decision
support was presented and visualised. The chapter emphasizes and illustrates and explains the case of:
risk stratification evidence not necessarily used in patients, but based on the fact that a set of markers
is present in the public data sets.

Engineering Standards and Design
Challenges
For the proposed CKD risk prediction framework, the engineering standard as well as the
societal,ethical issues are discussed here, along with the project management and financial analysis of
the project. Complex engineering mapping of the project is also discussed.
5.1
Compliance with the Standards
Not all standards are listed, only those relevant to this CKD risk prediction research project are
discussed. The section introduces software tools, computing environment and data-handling practices
that actually exist in the implemented system. If applicable, the alternatives are identified and why the
chosen alternative is preferred is provided. The decision support system to take place in the
production hospital is defined as a reproducible research system instead of a validated medical-device
and should not be bound by regulatory standards of medical-device hospital deployment; those
standards are not addressed in this final-year design project.
5.1.1
Software Standards
With Python 3.12 and PEP 8 conventions, most processing, logistic regression, and tree baselines,
calibration, and evaluation are written within the main MIMIC branch; PyTorch provides the ability to
add tabular MLP and ResMLP baselines and XGBoost provides the ability to add gradient-boosted
baselines. All metrics, SHAP outputs and any frozen model artifacts are stored in CSV, JSON and
pickle. The locked primary is explainable using SHAP. Fixed random seeds, grouped splits, modular
pipelines of branches and a fixed reporting lock ensure that the reproducibility is maintained. There
was no use of any proprietary tools or LIME, or R-only stacks.
5.1.2
Hardware Standards
The system was designed, and tested on a regularly used undergraduate research workstation. The
main hospitals classifier is a simple tabular classifier, dependent upon no specialised accelerator
equipment. The main pipeline can still be implemented on the central processing unit as heavier
neural baselines would benefit from the use of graphics processing if available. The need for
computation mainly comes from the support of big data collected locally from various public or
research clinical and wearable devices. No special cardiac sensor or dedicated clinical or hospital
computers were used.
5.1.3
Communication Standards
Data flows through as abstract research products in the form of structured data from preprocessing
through modelling, evaluation and demonstration. Clinical and wearable inputs are sourced from
public datasets to tabular feature representations, multimodal integration is done at branch probability
level and not by a shared patient record system. The decision-support prototype is a local research
interface that loads the precomputed model outputs for demonstration. No live hospital interface with
the application programming interfaces or full interoperability with the electronic health records has
been undertaken yet and will be incorporated into future work.

5.2
Impact on Society, Environment and Sustainability
5.2.1
Impact on Life
The clinicians can use the framework to reflect on the CKD risk at the time of an admission using the
calibrated probabilities and provide an explanation why using the SHAP explanation that may be
helpful in implementing earlier follow-up testing if demonstrated in the future. It is not designed for
the diagnostic of CKD nor is it a substitute for either eGFR-based or guidance-based assessment
either, but of course indirectly helpful to patients by better informed clinical review and care [2],[18].
However, there is no package deployed to healthcare providers as it is only a research prototype for
explainer presentation of risks. The remote monitoring user is not directly served, as the wearable
branch is not a WESAD stress test branch, and has not been validated to detect renal stress [5].
5.2.2
Impact on Society & Environment
While it can be evidence-based for digital-health research to gauge early awareness of CKD or
investigate potential opportunities for leveraging routine hospital data as a tool to understand
outcomes or broaden access for those with the disease, it does not move the needle necessarily on
improved outcomes or broadened access to health care. At this time, MIMIC-IV can only be used by
credentialed researchers [6].
If there is impact, then the computer work, process the data set, train and report was mostly done
locally, on the workstation. In comparison to large deep models, the locked primary model has a low
energy. The cost for resources is for the storage of large clinical files. No need for deployment either
in-lab or on-site was necessary.
5.2.3
Ethical Aspects
No patient records were specifically collected for this project and any datasets used are publicly
available and have been de-identified, in compliance to the requirements of research access [6], [16].
Bias can occur due to the strong effect on age, insurance, race and way of admission. SHAP and a
calibration reporting as proposed in [15] and [48] provides explainability. A disclaimer, ‘Research
Only' is included in the prototype and should not be used for the purposes of providing treatment
advice. The tool is for decision support only and clinical accountability is with the clinician [42], [49].
5.2.4
Sustainability Plan
Modular branches and frozen artifacts enable the independent update of data, models and explanations.
The main model is not very costly to operate for research purposes; some other required things for
EHR use in a hospital would not be performed here such as security, governance, and live EHR use.
Future: any iteration of MIMIC, new surveys, or new cohorts of wearable and EHR could be added in
a way that is repeatedly validated, recalibrated, and SHAP reviewed.

Chapter 6
Conclusion
This chapter gives a summary of the work done in this research, discusses the limitations of the
proposed CKD risk prediction framework and points the way for future work. It is proposed to be a
research framework to be used as a tool to help with a decision and not a separate clinical diagnostic
system.
6.1
Summary
Combining population clinical data (NHANES) with multimodal, wrist wearable physiological data
(WESAD stress proxy) as well as multimodal data late fusion via probability level and SHAP
explainability were explored as ancillary research decision support to the multimodal CKD risk
prediction system. The Primary locked model which was sigmoid calibrated logistic regression
classifier for MIMIC admission had a test AUROC of 0.761, F1 accuracy metric value of 0.415,
sensitivity of 0.674, specificity of 0.696 and ECE value of 0.020 when threshold value was set at 0.16.
This provided a similar level of discrimination to uncalibrated and treelike approaches as above, but
with significantly better probability reliability measures; uncalibrated logistic regression discriminated
about as well AUROC ~0.761, ECE 0.249 while treelike approaches of about the same quality
achieved a somewhat higher AUROC ~0.768 but much lower probability reliability measures. test
discrimination was high for NHANES logistic regression in separate cohorts and the AUROC value
for proxy task was 0.764 for WESAD cohort. This was because it was considered to be a late fusion,
and with no evidence of patient level multimodal CKD benefit proxy AUROC ~0.50. Temporal
validation and grouping was done but MIMIC discrimination was not affected. Key risk drivers were
found to be: age, insurance, race and admission context
by the global SHAP Analysis. The risk
values that were calculated were plotted in streamlit for MIMIC branch. Rather the project overall
results is an explainable pipeline risk-modelling for admission calibrated for public research data for
research decision support not in actual clinical practice.
6.2
Limitation
While the framework exhibits valuable research evidence, there are some limitations of the framework.

Dataset limitations: The evidence for the primary hospitals is obtained from only one centre
(MIMIC-IV). Public datasets such as NHANES, MIMIC-IV and WESAD are not overlapping,
meaning that no same patient multimodal fusion can be validated. WESAD does not have any
CKD labelling, and only has a stress-versus-baseline physiology proxy. NHANES is based on a
single cycle survey design and a single visit for the labelling of CKD instead of confirmed
longitudinal disease. The laboratory coverage in the admission window is less with restrictions
on availability of creatinine in MIMIC.

Model limitations: MIMIC discrimination is moderate, but the screening operating point is at
the cost of a high number of false positive cases. Limited search for hyperparameters was
performed and there was no external benchmark run on the same splits with published CKD
systems. Late fusion does not support acquisition of cross-modal processing of features. The
wearable and fusion limbs do not yield renal outcome prediction benefit for individual patients.

Computational limitations: The tables in the bigger hospital are voluminous and need a lot of
local storage and processing. Primary model is CPU-feasible, optional GPUs are used for mainly
neural baselines and for branch training. No production level serving or monitoring system was
provided.

Clinical limitations: The system was never deployed or prospective evaluated and not expertly


============================================================
PAGE 53
============================================================
43
reviewed in practice. MIMIC labels are based on ICD proxies, and not KDIGO staging and
follow-up. Outputs will be limited to risk estimates in the assistance of research decision making
and should not be used as a single source for diagnosis or treatment suggestions.

Explainability limitations: SHAP attributions do not include the mechanics of any association,
just only statistical. There can be coding and socioeconomic bias in the explanations, which rely
on the manner in which information is documented in the sending hospital. The fitted linear
model refers to the local distribution of data from clinical settings, and local explanations are
only valid for this case.
6.3
Future Work
Future work should be continued down realistic lines existing and based on evidence; with expansion
of the current implementation.

Aligned multimodal cohorts: Collect or synthesize datasets in which the clinical records and
wearable sensors are the same patients, with the sole idea being that of exploring the usefulness
of early fusion of data and multimodal benefit at the patient level.

Prospective and external clinical validation: Test the hospital (admission) model on external
databases, future workflows, as well as on separate hospitals, while at the same time get familiar
with the temporal checking able within MIMIC.

Richer hospital modalities: Add other physiological and time series information other than the
many tabular data at the admission level like the waveform data available in the extended
versions of MIMIC.

Fairness and bias mitigation: Do auditing from the various the sub-populations with varying
demographic features, insurance coverage and documentation and investigate fairness-aware
training and threshold-based policies.

Clinical integration: Set up interoperability and workflow integration with standards-based
approach with nephrology and hospital stakeholders and try out clinician in the loop.

Operational deployment research: Avoid conducting any pilot outside of the research context
not featuring the calibration drift monitoring, model governance and versioning.
These directions taken together may take the framework from a research prototype into one that is
responsibly designed and calibrated as a decision-support tool.
